Around Lake Stevens, an open gutter is a part-time job. Fir and cedar needles knit into a mat,
maple seeds and blossoms pile up in spring, and roof grit washes down all winter. Most gutter
"protection" on the market can't handle that combination. After trying the usual options, the one
we now put on customers' homes is **Raptor stainless steel micro-mesh** — and here is the
reasoning.

## The three ways gutter guards fail here

**Foam inserts.** They fill the gutter with a sponge. Fine debris and pollen work into the foam,
it stays wet, and within a year or two you have moss and weeds growing *in your gutter*. In a damp
climate they are a maintenance headache, not a solution.

**Reverse-curve ("helmet") systems.** These rely on surface tension to pull water around a curved
nose and into a slot. In a light rain they work. In a Pacific Northwest downpour, water shoots
straight past the nose and over the front — and the open slot still lets in needles and grit. They
also bolt to the roof, which can void a shingle warranty.

**Plastic and aluminum screens.** The holes are far too big to stop fir needles. They keep out
maple leaves and tennis balls; everything finer sifts right through and packs the trough anyway.

## What Raptor is

Raptor is a **contractor-grade micro-mesh** guard. The filtering surface is a
**Type 304 stainless steel mesh with roughly 900 openings per square inch**, set into an
**aluminum frame** with a patented raised **V-bend** down the length of each panel.

The V-bend does two jobs. It adds rigidity so the panel doesn't oil-can or sag between hangers,
and it keeps flat debris — leaves, blossom, needle mats — from lying flush against the mesh where
it can seal off water. Debris that lands on Raptor tends to dry out, get light, and blow or wash
off.

## Does micro-mesh really keep up with our rain?

This is the first question every customer asks, and it's a fair one. The short answer: yes,
because the mesh has **far more open area than the gutter itself can carry away**. Even when part
of the surface is covered with debris, the remaining mesh passes more water than the downspouts
can drain.

The failure mode people worry about — water sheeting *over* the front edge in a hard rain — is a
reverse-curve problem, not a micro-mesh one. Raptor's front trough is shaped to grab that sheet of
water and turn it down into the gutter. In our experience on real Snohomish County roofs, through
real November storms, it stays ahead of the water.

## How we install it

Raptor is sold as a DIY product, and a handy homeowner can absolutely do it. What a professional
install buys you is the prep and the finish:

1. **Assess and measure.** We check gutter condition, slope, fascia and downspouts, and flag any
   rotten or pulled-away sections *before* quoting.
2. **Clean and re-pitch.** Existing gutters are hand-cleared, flushed, re-sealed at the seams and
   re-pitched toward the downspouts. Guards on top of a gutter that doesn't drain just hide the
   problem.
3. **Install the Raptor guard.** Panels are cut to length, lapped at the seams, trimmed to the
   roofline and fastened with Raptor's stainless screws — never staples, never adhesive-only.
4. **Flow test.** We run water across every run and confirm full flow through each downspout
   before we pack up.

It's a one-visit job on most homes, and it
[bundles well with a roof soft wash](/services/roof-cleaning) — you don't want years of loosened
moss washing onto brand-new guards.

## What changes after install

You stop [cleaning your gutters](/services/gutter-cleaning) twice a year. That's the point.

You're not completely hands-off forever — over several years, fine dust and pollen can build a thin
film on top of the mesh, and a quick rinse from a ladder or a soft wash clears it. But the annual
ritual of scooping wet needle sludge out of a trough in the rain is done.
