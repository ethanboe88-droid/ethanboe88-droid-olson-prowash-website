Short answer: **most homes west of the Cascades should have the roof cleaned every 2 to 3 years.**
That's tighter than the "every 4–6 years" advice you'll read from national sources, and the reason
is simple — those sources aren't dealing with our climate.

## Why the Pacific Northwest is different

The <a href="https://www.weather.gov/sew/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">National Weather Service in Seattle</a> tracks what we all feel:
a long, cool, wet season from October through May where surfaces rarely dry out. Combine that
with heavy tree cover and short winter days, and you get near-perfect conditions for moss, algae
and lichen. Washington State University's
<a href="https://hortsense.cahnr.wsu.edu/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Hortsense</a> resource frames moss as a symptom of shade and
moisture — and in the PNW, most roofs have both.

A roof in Phoenix might go a decade between cleanings. A roof in Lake Stevens under a couple of
Douglas firs can grow visible moss in a single winter.

## A realistic schedule

| Roof situation | Clean every |
| --- | --- |
| Open exposure, full sun, few trees | 3 years |
| Typical suburban lot, some afternoon shade | 2–3 years |
| Heavy tree cover, north-facing slopes, near water | 1.5–2 years |
| Already had thick moss removed | Re-treat at 2 years to stay ahead of it |

A [roof soft wash](/services/roof-cleaning) done on this kind of schedule is maintenance — it
keeps the roof clear and the shingles protected. Waiting until the moss is thick turns it into a
bigger, more expensive job, and by then the shingles have already taken damage.

## Factors that change how often you need it

**Shade.** The single biggest one. North slopes and tree-shaded roofs stay wet and re-grow moss
two to three times faster than sunny exposures.

**Tree debris.** Fir needles, leaves and seed litter trap moisture and feed growth. A roof that
gets swept or blown off a couple times a year stays cleaner longer.

**Pitch.** Steeper roofs shed water and debris better and dry faster. Low-slope sections, porch
roofs and valleys hold everything and grow the most moss.

**Roof age and type.** Older, weathered asphalt has a rougher surface that moss grips easily. Newer
shingles and metal stay clean longer.

**Whether it was killed or just knocked off last time.** A proper treatment kills the moss at the
root and buys 2–3 years. A scrape-and-blow job regrows in months.

## Signs your roof is overdue right now

- Green fuzz or cushions of moss along the shingle edges and in the valleys
- Black streaks running down the slopes (that's algae, and it spreads)
- Moss or grass growing *in the gutters*
- Granules collecting in the gutters and at the downspout splash blocks
- Shingle edges visibly lifting or curling where moss has crept underneath
- It's been more than 3 years and you can't remember the last cleaning

If you're seeing lifted shingles, don't wait for the calendar — that's water getting under the
roof. Our post on [what happens if you don't clean your roof](/blog/what-happens-if-you-dont-clean-roof)
walks through how that progresses.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I stretch it to 4 or 5 years?**
On a sunny, open roof, maybe. On a shaded PNW roof, you'll pay for it in shingle life. The cost of
a cleaning is a rounding error next to a roof replacement.

**Does cleaning the roof more often wear it out?**
No — a low-pressure [soft wash](/services/soft-washing) is gentle enough to repeat indefinitely.
It's *pressure washing* and *moss left to dig in* that wear a roof out.

**What's the best time of year?**
Late spring or early fall. See
[when is the best time to clean your roof in Washington](/blog/best-time-clean-roof-washington-state).

**Should I get on a plan?**

schedule at a lower per-visit rate and folds in gutter clean-outs.
